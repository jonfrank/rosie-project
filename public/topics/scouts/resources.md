# Scouts Teacher Guide

## Session aims

- Students will learn about the Scouts during World War Two through active investigation of primary source materials. 
- They will use the investigation questions to think in depth about each object before seeing further information, developing their critical thinking skills.
- Students will bring their learning together by completing the activity at the end of the session.

## Topic Introduction

The Scouts movement began in the early 20th Century, aiming to equip the youth of Britain with skills which would be useful in the British military. The founder of the Scouts, Robert Baden-Powell, was a military hero in Edwardian Britain for his role in the South African War (also known as the Second Boer War). In 1908 he published his 'Scouting for Boys' handbook, which focused on a variety of outdoor activities such as woodwork, camping and exploring, as well as first aid, duties of the Scouts as citizens, and healthy habits. The handbook included many other topics as well, and was the beginning of Scout movements across Britain and the British Empire. Originally, girls joined Scout groups, but when the Boy Scouts Association was founded in 1910 this was no longer accepted, so the Girl Guides Association was created. The name changed to The Scout Association in 1967, and girls were allowed to join the oldest group from 1976 and all age groups from 1991.

+++[Scouting and Empire - Additional Context and Debates]
The history of Scouting is deeply intertwined in the history of the British Empire, and is complex and contested by historians. Robert Baden-Powell, while a hero in Britain during his lifetime, is a controversial figure today. Some have argued that his intentions when creating the handbook were reflective of the fears of the Edwardian elite, including military weakness, and that the movement was an attempt to establish social control, while others would simply claim that it was an innovative way to train future generations and produce happier citizens. The [handbook](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/65993/65993-h/65993-h.htm#part5) contains a section titled 'Our Empire: How it Grew - How it Must be Held', which explains to the children how their 'forefathers worked hard, fought hard, and died hard, to make this Empire for you. Don't let them look down from heaven, and see you loafing about with hands in your pockets, doing nothing to keep it up'. The messages of colonialism and empire within this handbook, combined with Baden-Powell's actions as a colonial British officer, make him a challenging and controversial figure. If you're interested in looking into this history in more detail, make sure to look at the 'Robert Baden-Powell Debates' section of the Further Resources tab at the bottom of this page.

**Scouts at War**

The Boy Scouts Association grew and grew throughout the early 20th Century, and [by 1940 over 53,000 Scouts were trained up and undertaking jobs](https://www.scouts.org.uk/about-us/our-history/our-online-exhibitions/scouting-during-the-second-world-war/scouting-on-the-home-front-they-were-prepared/) to help with the war effort. The jobs that Scouts did were varied and hugely important to keeping Britain going during World War Two. 

**Evacuation:** Some older Scouts helped with the evacuation of younger children, looking after the children and carrying luggage. Scouts were sometimes evacuated in their Troops, giving them some familiarity.

**Air Raid Precautions:** Scouts in the cities would help local ARP wardens prepare for blackouts by painting white lines along the edges of roads for visibility, as well as helping households build air raid shelters. This could involved constructing a Morrison shelter within a family’s home, or helping to dig an Anderson Shelter into their garden. 

**Air Raids:** During air raids, especially during the Blitz, Scouts were called upon to support volunteer organisations including the ARP, fire and ambulance services. Many Scouts were messengers, carrying information between different services when the phone lines went down during an air raid. Some Scouts worked as fire watchers, keeping lookout and alerting the authorities to any blazes, as well as running buckets of water and sand to the firefighters and sometimes putting out the fires themselves. On occasion, Scouts were sent out to look for unexploded incendiary bombs which needed to be found and disposed of. Scouts also worked as stretcher-bearers, first-aiders, and even hospital staff to get sick children to safer locations during air raids. The work which Scouts undertook during air raids was dangerous but vital, and they helped to keep many people safe. 

**Resourcefulness:** Scouts got involved all aspects of the war effort on the Home Front, including the 'Dig For Victory' campaign to grow as much food as possible. Some Scouts acted as Junior Salvage Stewards - you can learn more about them in [Investigation 2](/topic/junior-salvage-stewards/resources) if you haven't already done so. Younger Scouts could do these jobs too, as they were less dangerous and required less physical strength. Scouts had been trained in many skills which would be useful in the event of an enemy invasion of Britain, so they were recruited to teach members of the Home Guard skills such as first aid and bushcraft.

Scouts of all ages were able to get involved with the war effort on the Home Front, and their work was vital to keeping the country functioning during World War Two. 

## Using the Investigation Videos

- For each new object, play the video once before revealing the investigation questions. This will encourage the students to use their own knowledge and observations to start thinking about the object.
- Before revealing the first investigation question for the object, you could ask students to share what they notice about the object with the person next to them, or the people on their table.
- Use simple prompt questions such as 'what do you see in the video?' and see what they come up with.
- Each object has a series of investigation questions. Some of these are questions followed by additional information, where it is important for students to have additional knowledge before moving on.
- There is no requirement to cover every question in detail. If one object question sparks a great discussion, stick with it, and if an object is not grabbing your students' interest, feel free to move through the questions at a faster pace.

## Activity

**Design a badge for a job that Scouts did during World War Two.**

Encourage students to think about the different jobs they've looked at in the session. Was there one that particularly stood out to them? 

If your students need some inspiration, you could look at [this page](https://www.scoutcollecting.co.uk/ssshop4-badges-scouts-skills__amp__proficiency_493-war_issue_1939_to_1945859.html) which has images of a vast selection of World War Two Scout badges. The page also gives information about the requirements for earning many of the badges. 

There are two template options for this activity. One contains simple circles which can be cut out and designed, and the other also contains the 'Boy Scout' lettering around the edge of the badge to give the students a starting point. Download either or both below.

[Simple circle template](scouts-worksheet.pdf)

[Circle with lettering template](scouts-worksheet-lettering.pdf)

If you have the resources, you could source a badge making kit to make badges that students can pin on and wear. Alternatively, you could use fabric, fabric pens and safety pins to achieve a similar, more authentic outcome.
