# Junior Salvage Stewards Teacher Guide

## Session aims

- Students will learn about the role of children as Junior Salvage Stewards during World War Two through active investigation of primary source materials.
- They will use the investigation questions to think in depth about each object **before** seeing further information, developing their critical thinking skills.
- Students will bring their learning together by completing the activity at the end of the session.

## Topic Introduction

In this session, students will learn about the role that children played as Junior Salvage Stewards, or 'Cogs', during World War Two.

During the war, demand for ordinary materials used in manufacturing skyrocketed. In December 1939, just a few months into the war, the National Salvage Scheme was set up to collect scrap materials from households. It was a government scheme largely facilitated by the Women’s Voluntary Services (WVS), whose members signed up in their thousands to promote the importance of salvage to the general public. 

At the end of 1940, the WVS introduced the Cog Scheme with the aim of 'interesting children in salvage work'. WVS members visited schools to give talks on salvage, and children who volunteered were appointed as Junior Salvage Stewards. They were known as 'Cogs' as they were small parts in the national salvage machine.

The main role of a Cog was to help with salvage collection efforts in their local area. A variety of materials were collected, and Cogs were required to know what could be collected and what it could be turned into. 

**Types of salvage:**

**Paper and cardboard** was fundamental to equipping the military. It was re-pulped and reused over and over again to make parts of bombs, shells and mines, as well as cartridges for guns, petrol containers, and targets for practice and training. Continuously re-pulping paper eventually reduced its quality, so new, high quality paper was found in the form of books. By October 1943, 56 million books had been collected for salvage.

**Bones** could be collected from any animal carcass, and the fats extracted to make glycerine to be used in high explosives. The fats were also used in soap, glue for woodwork and candles. Once the fats had been extracted the remains were used for cattle feed and crop fertiliser. 

**Rags and fabric scraps** were highly sought after, for numerous different reasons. Colourful rags, especially red ones, could be used to dye other fabrics, linen and calico were turned into charts and paper for the admiralty, and stiff white shirts and collars were turned into five pound notes. Knit fabric scraps were in particular demand as they could be used to make suits, uniforms and blankets, and even low quality fabric could be turned into mattress stuffing and roofing felt. 

**Metals** including aluminium, iron, copper, steel and lead were used to make military equipment such as aeroplanes, ships, tanks, guns and shells. The 'Pots to Planes' scheme was initiated by Lady Reading (founder and Chairman of the WVS), and there was a huge national response with households donating their saucepans to support the RAF. There is debate over how much of the metal collected from households was actually used, but the amount donated demonstrates the success of the WVS campaign.

**Rubber** was needed to produce new tyres for planes and army vehicles, as well as wellies for children, and even fire hoses. Worn out tyres were collected by Cogs, and WVS members fished for tyres in local ponds.

**Kitchen waste and food scraps** were collected to be boiled down and turned into pig swill to feed livestock.

**Being a Junior Salvage Steward:**

Children in the Cogs had to know what could be salvaged and what it could be used for. They were encouraged to help their mothers take salvage out for collection, or move it to the local collection depot. They were asked to act as 'Salvage Officers in their homes', giving them responsibility and a role in the war effort that they could take ownership over.    The Cogs even had a song called 'There’ll Always Be A Dustbin', sung to the tune of 'There’ll Always Be An England'.

After six weeks of volunteering as a Junior Salvage Steward, children were awarded a Cog badge in recognition of their efforts. Particularly spectacular efforts were celebrated in the local papers, and some members of the WVS even threw tea parties to thank their local Cogs. 

## Using the Investigation Videos

- For each new object, play the video once before revealing the investigation questions. This will encourage the students to use their own knowledge and observations to start thinking about the object.
- Before revealing the first investigation question for the object, you could ask students to share what they notice about the object with the person next to them, or the people on their table.
- Use simple prompt questions such as 'what do you see in the video?' and see what they come up with.
- Each object has a series of investigation questions. Some of these are questions followed by additional information, where it is important for students to have additional knowledge before moving on.
- There is no requirement to cover every question in detail. If one object question sparks a great discussion, stick with it, and if an object is not grabbing your students' interest, feel free to move through the questions at a faster pace.

## Activity

**Write a postcard to a friend encouraging them to join the Junior Salvage Stewards.**

Encourage students to think about the benefits of being a Cog. How will they be helping the war effort? What makes it fun? Is there a reward?

If you’ve covered persuasive writing already, you could use this activity to remind students of the techniques they learned about.  

If you have card available, print out [this template with two postcards per page](postcard-double.pdf). You can get the children to write their message in the template section, then turn it over and design the front of postcard.

If you are printing on paper, print out [this single postcard template](postcard-single.pdf). Students can fold the page in half and glue it down to create a thicker postcard which they can then write and design.

## Alternative Activity Suggestions

- Design a badge for recycling champions at your school, using the Cogs’ badge as inspiration. You could do this on paper, fabric, or even use air-drying clay to sculpt them!
- Have a salvage competition: using either recycling collected from other classes in the school or things collected from home, split your students into teams and challenge them to make the most useful thing out of the 'salvage'. A bit like junk modelling, but with a focus on creating something with purpose. You could even get students to pitch their creations to the rest of the class, and have a vote on the most useful. This is great if you want to challenge their creativity and develop their public speaking skills. 

## SEN Adaptations

To make the session more tactile and sensory friendly, you could source some of these objects to use alongside the investigation videos:
- Replica gas mask boxes are cheap and easy to get hold of, and will give your students something tactile when looking at the gas masks. See one example here: https://www.therationroom.co.uk/product/ww2-childs-gas-mask-box-air-raid-prop-with-london-borough-id-label 
- Replica clothing ration books can also be purchased online. This one comes with a food ration book, which can be used in the Women’s Land Army Session: https://www.therationroom.co.uk/product/1940s-ww2-replica-ration-book-clothing-book-and-ration-book-envelope 
- Print out the ‘Join the ‘Cogs’’ poster and let your students annotate it in small groups. This is particularly good if you want to stretch and challenge some of your students, as they can get up close to it and really think about what each detail means.
- Collect a selection of everyday materials which were collected in salvage drives, and let your students look at and feel them, in addition to seeing the shrapnel video. These could be things like paper and cardboard, rags/scraps of fabric, things made from rubber, and even potato peelings!

## Further Resources

- [Salvage on the Home Front](https://www.royalvoluntaryservice.org.uk/media/hiqpms3j/salvage_on_the_home_front_2021.pdf) - The most comprehensive source of information about the Junior Salvage Stewards. This article was produced by the Royal Voluntary Service Heritage Collection and is thoroughly researched and supported by primary sources.

- [Clothes Rationing in Britain: Imperial War Museums](https://www.iwm.org.uk/history/8-facts-about-clothes-rationing-in-britain-during-the-second-world-war) - 8 facts about clothes rationing in Britain during World War Two, each supported by an object from IWM’s online collections.

- [WW2 People’s War Archive - BBC: Tony Greenfield’s Story](https://www.bbc.co.uk/history/ww2peopleswar/stories/33/a7889133.shtml) - Tony was signed up to be a salvage steward by his dad just before he turned 12. His experiences of World War Two might be of interest to students who enjoy history.

- [WW2 People’s War Archive - BBC: John Stuart Newcomb](https://www.bbc.co.uk/history/ww2peopleswar/stories/53/a6144653.shtml) - John was a Cog, and also enjoyed plane spotting and listening to foreign radio stations. He describes rationing, air-raids and gas masks, as well as his role as a Junior Salvage Steward.

- [Ten Years’ Work: 1938-1948](https://www.royalvoluntaryservice.org.uk/media/qxfhkf5u/ten_years_work_2021.pdf) - A WVS report published in 1948, looking back on the service’s role in the war effort. This report explains how they kept children interested in the work throughout the war.

- [You Can Help Your Country: English Children’s Work During the Second World War](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctv17ppc3b) - An interesting book explaining many different roles that children held during World War Two, including the Junior Salvage Stewards in Chapter 5: 'Younger children’s work: Doing their bit'.

- [A Look at Salvage and Recycling on the Home Front](https://www.thehistoricalfictioncompany.com/post/there-ll-always-be-a-dustbin-a-look-at-salvage-and-recycling-on-the-home-front-by-susanna-bavin) - An easy-to-read article about the salvage campaigns during World War Two.

- ['We Want Everybody’s Salvage!': Recycling, Voluntarism, and the People’s War](https://eprints.leedsbeckett.ac.uk/id/eprint/5488/1/WeWantEverybodysSalvageAM-IRVING.pdf) - An academic article examining the role of volunteers in the British government’s salvage campaign.
